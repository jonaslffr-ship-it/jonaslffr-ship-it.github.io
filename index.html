What the amber line on the surface is

The at-the-money line (strike = forward) across all expiries — the ATM term structure. Its slope is contango (rising) or backwardation (falling), analysed below.

Term structure: contango, backwardation, calendar arbitrage

Contango — longer-dated options carry more implied vol than shorter ones — is the calm-market norm; backwardation (front above back) marks stress or an event close ahead. Neither is an arbitrage. That only arises if total implied variance w = σ²·T falls with maturity: then a calendar spread (sell the front, buy the back) would cost less than nothing.

Size: how many vol points the back expiry’s IV at that strike would have to rise for w to stop falling. A violation smaller than the two fits’ own RMSE cannot be told apart from fit noise; even larger ones are rarely tradable once bid–ask spreads are paid.

Calendar spreads and the curve. A long calendar sells the front expiry and buys the back one at the same strike: long vega, short the front’s faster time decay. In contango it pays the richer back-month vol; in backwardation it sells the richer front — it then gains if the curve normalizes and loses if the stress persists. ② The math, step by step

Everything on this page follows from eight small ideas. The live numbers in grey come from today’s data.

1 · From annual to daily volatility

Volatility indices are quoted as annualized standard deviations. If daily returns are independent, variances add, so volatility grows with the square root of time. With 252 trading days a year:

$$\sigma_{\text{day}}=\frac{\mathrm{IV}}{\sqrt{252}}\approx\frac{\mathrm{IV}}{15.87},\qquad \sigma_{h\text{ days}}=\mathrm{IV}\sqrt{\frac{h}{252}}$$

Fine print: the VIX annualizes 30 calendar days with a 365-day year. Spread over the ~21 trading days in 30 calendar days that is VIX² · 30/365/21 ≈ VIX²/256 per trading day — the ÷√252 rule is within about 1 %.

2 · The expected move and the ladder

One standard deviation of the one-day return, in points, is the expected move (EM). The ladder is the anchor plus or minus multiples of it:

$$\mathrm{EM}=P_0\,\sigma_{\text{day}},\qquad L_{\pm k}=P_0\,(1\pm k\,\sigma_{\text{day}})=P_0\pm k\,\mathrm{EM}$$

3 · What the normal distribution promises

If the one-day return R were normal with standard deviation σ, the close would land inside ±kσ with probability

$$\Pr\big(|R|\le k\sigma\big)=2\Phi(k)-1$$

Φ is the standard normal distribution function: 38.3 % inside ±0.5σ, 68.3 % inside ±1σ, 95.4 % inside ±2σ. Section ③ checks this against five years of closes.

4 · Touching is twice as likely as closing beyond

Reflection principle: for a driftless random walk, every path that touches the level and ends above it has a mirror image — reflected at the first touch — that ends below it. So

$$\Pr\Big(\max_{t\le 1}W_t\ge k\Big)=2\,\Pr\big(W_1\ge k\big)=2\big(1-\Phi(k)\big)$$

+1σ is touched on 31.7 % of days (each side), although the close ends beyond it on only 15.9 %.

5 · The 50 % benchmark for “levels hold”

The same symmetry says: once the level is touched, the rest of the day is a fresh random walk starting at the level. It closes back inside half of the time:

$$\Pr\Big(W_1<k\ \Big|\ \max_{t\le1}W_t\ge k\Big)=1-\frac{1-\Phi(k)}{2\,(1-\Phi(k))}=\frac12$$

A level that “held” is only interesting if it holds clearly more often than 50 % — pure noise already produces a turn at every other touched level. Try it in the simulator below; test it in the level lab ④.

6 · Fair value of the future

Put–call parity at the futures expiry links calls C, puts P, the discount factor D and the forward F. Regressing C − P on the strike K gives D (minus the slope) and F:

$$C(K)-P(K)=D\,(F-K),\qquad \text{basis}=F-S$$

7 · Why the ruler is usually too wide

Option sellers demand a premium for bearing variance risk, so implied variance tends to exceed the variance that is later realized — the variance risk premium:

$$\mathrm{VRP}_t=\mathrm{IV}_t^2-\mathbb{E}_t\big[\mathrm{RV}_{t,t+21}^2\big]\;>\;0\ \text{on average}$$

8 · Where the rule breaks

  • VIX and VXN measure 30 calendar days. Using them for one day assumes a flat term structure; VIX1D prices the next day directly.
  • Scheduled events (CPI, FOMC, earnings) concentrate variance on single days.
  • Returns have fat tails and a skew: large down days are more frequent than the normal allows.
  • Daily bars cannot tell whether the high came before the low — so this page never claims an order of events it cannot see.

    How not to fool yourself with a backtest

    1. Fill limit orders on a trade-through, not a touch.
    2. Never let a bar’s high and low both be “known” after an intrabar fill.
    3. Resolve take-profit vs. stop in time order — or assume the worst.
    4. Charge realistic costs, and count every configuration you tried.
    5. Treat a Sharpe above 3 on a simple rule as a bug report.

    Research 1 · working paper v1.1

    Forecasting holds up. The strategy doesn’t.

    I forecast the daily realized variance of the S&P 500 — built from 5-minute returns on 1-minute data, February 2005 to August 2026, out of sample from January 2013 (3,422 trading days) — and test whether those forecasts can size equity exposure. Hypotheses, parameter grids, costs and, decisively, disqualification rules were fixed in writing before the first run; all 73 configurations computed enter the multiple-testing correction.

    Findings

    • Volatility is forecastable. MSE-based OOS R² +23 % at one day, +27 % at one week, +43 % at one month (the one-month gain is not significant at 5 %, Clark–West p = 0.053).
    • Implied volatility carries information past realized volatility does not (HAR-IV vs. log-HAR, Clark–West p = 0.019, in both sub-periods).
    • Calibrated on average (Mincer–Zarnowitz slope ≈ 1): of the 2.54× under-estimation on spike days, a perfectly calibrated forecaster already produces ≈ 1.90× through outcome selection under right skew.
    • The strategy is disqualified: PBO 0.84, single market, Sharpe advantage inside the bootstrap noise. Published anyway.

    What this does not show

    • A tradeable edge — the contribution is the forecasting result and the validation method.
    • Anything beyond the S&P 500; the strategy result is single-market by construction.
    • That a rich feature set helps robustly — its QLIKE gain does not carry over to MSE.
    • Live performance: nothing counts as “supported” without a live prediction log — that is what the daily forecast is for.

    Research 2 · in progress · pre-registration first

    How much volatility does the options market price for a single day?

    Since 2022, S&P 500 options expire every trading day. Because SPXW contracts settle on the closing print, a 0DTE chain observed at 09:35 ET prices exactly the variance of the rest of the session. I’m building a model-free measure of it from second-level quotes on every strike: 1,771 expiration days, January 2018 to September 2026 — five years before Cboe’s VIX1D — and comparing it with realized variance over the same window.

    No results yet — by design. Hypotheses, effect sizes from the literature, the sample split and every test will be committed publicly before the first result is computed. Try the estimator →

    QuestionHypothesis or gate
    RQ1 · Measures what it should?Gate: tracks VIX1D where both exist (since 2023) — high rank correlation, no drift in the ratio. If it fails, fix the measurement first.
    RQ2 · Intraday variance risk premium?E[IVoc − RVoc] > 0; change with daily expiries (2022); FOMC/CPI days.
    RQ3 · Does it forecast?IVoc vs. HAR + VIX for same-day RV, out of sample (QLIKE, Clark–West, MCS).

    Known limits, stated up front

    • Spreads are wide after the open — measurement time is a robustness dimension (09:35 vs. 10:00).
    • Before 2022, 0DTE days are mostly Mon/Wed/Fri — not a random sample; the 2022 break is tested explicitly.
    • With jumps, the log-contract replication is not exactly quadratic variation.
    • The quote data are licensed and will never be published — only code, a synthetic sample, aggregates and figures.

    Integrity as a feature

    Research standards

    1. Pre-registration first.Hypotheses, effect sizes, sample splits and decision rules are committed before the first run.
    2. Out of sample, or it didn’t happen.Nothing is called “validated” without an out-of-sample test.
    3. Count every trial.Multiple testing is corrected — deflated Sharpe ratio, probability of backtest overfitting.
    4. Direction check.The sign of the data must match the sign of the claim before the claim is made.
    5. Reproducible by default.Clone, install, run — the printed numbers must match.
    6. Errata, not silent edits.Corrections are logged publicly.
    7. Negative results are results.They are published with the same care.
    8. Licensed data stays licensed.Only code, derived aggregates and data fingerprints are published.

    1. One question first.Every project starts as a single falsifiable question with an expected effect size — not as a search for a good backtest.
    2. Commit before I look.Hypotheses, sample splits, costs and kill rules go into a pre-registration before the first run; the commit timestamp is the proof.
    3. Test it like it’s wrong.Out-of-sample or walk-forward only, every trial counted, estimators checked against a known answer before they touch real data.
    4. Publish all of it.Code that reproduces from a clean clone, negative results included, corrections logged as errata instead of silent edits.

    The full rules are in the research standards.
